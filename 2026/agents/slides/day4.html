A tool is a JSON schema and a function

{"name": "search",
 "description": "Full-text search over the Dvin corpus.",
 "parameters": {
   "type": "object",
   "properties": {
     "query": {"type": "string"},
     "k":     {"type": "integer"}},
   "required": ["query"]}}

The description is prompt. It is the only thing telling the model when to reach for this rather than something else.

Students underrate this. Tool descriptions are load-bearing text, and improving them is one of the cheapest wins in milestone 3.

Why the model can't just get it wrong

The server constrains decoding to the grammar of a valid call.

Your judge needs a judge

  1. Write the rubric: the rule that says what counts as a correct answer. "Names the right department, and quotes the current fee."
  2. Two people apply it to real cases from your runs, independently.
  3. Compute Cohen's κ between those two people.
  4. Only if they agree, point the LLM judge at the same cases and score it against their labels.
κwhat it means
0.0they agreed exactly as often as two coin flips would
0.6the usual floor for trusting a rubric
1.0perfect agreement

If two humans cannot agree with your rubric,
the rubric is broken. Not the judge.

κ is agreement corrected for chance: raw percentage agreement flatters you badly on skewed labels, where both raters say "correct" most of the time anyway. Trajectory judging adds biases text judging does not have, including argument blindness, failing to notice a wrong value passed to a tool. BabelJudge, arXiv:2606.22329, on judge reliability across languages and trajectories.

Define κ out loud, the same way you did pass^k. And say why step 3 comes last: measuring a judge against labels two people could not agree on tells you nothing.

Where the interval comes from

compute the accuracy of each resample
sort them; take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles
